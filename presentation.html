1 · Setup

One time step, and how we measure it

Share of a step for v1 at N = 5 000, P = 24 (C2).

  • Hardware: Xeon E5-2650 v4, 24 cores per node, up to two nodes (P = 48); Open MPI over UCX.
  • Correct before fast: every scheme against the serial run for N = 5, 100, 1 000 and P = 1 to 24: 228 of 228 runs agree, at most 1.3·10⁻¹³ relative.
  • Fair comparisons: the same tiled force kernel everywhere, 3 repeats and their median, the serial run and every P of a sweep on the same node.
  • One node is faster: n-62-21-75 is 12 % faster with the same CPU. Every time is corrected with a factor per node from its serial runs.

2 · Strong scaling · Amdahl

Why: more ranks only shrink the part that can be split

Amdahl's law splits a time step into a fixed part, which stays the same however many ranks there are, and the rest, which shrinks like 1/P. Each bar is one time step: the more ranks, the shorter the step, and the larger the fixed part's share.

4 · Communication schemes

Why the ring loses at small N: it calls the kernel P times

One time step with 24 ranks on one node, to scale. v1 gathers every position once and calls the force kernel once. The ring passes the blocks on 23 times and calls the kernel once per block, and every call costs a fixed amount of Python, however small the block.

5 · In depth: overlap

The ring can send the next block while it computes. It does not help.

C4 ran the ring with and without overlap: no gain on one node, and on two nodes up to 10 % slower at small N. Two ranks and one message at a time show why.

5 · In depth: overlap · the idea

In the ring every block visits every rank, and could travel while the tiles are computed

—

Drawn as overlap is meant to work, for P = 4 and 4 tiles per block, not to scale. In the real runs at N = 5 000 and P = 48 a block is 105 stars (2.5 KB); it travels in about 7 µs, while computing with it takes about 1.2 ms.

5 · In depth: overlap · our runs

In the real runs there is almost nothing to hide

A perfect overlap can save at most the transfers themselves, (P − 1) · Tcomm per step. Next to the computation that is tiny. What the overlap ring did get is the cost of its extra calls.

6 · In depth: memory

Every rank holds the whole catalogue, whatever the scheme

Peak after loading, P = 24, from 1 000 stars up to the whole catalogue (589 993 after the subset filter). The ring and 2D drop the full positions only after the peak: afterwards they hold 36 and 46 B per star instead of 58.

6 · In depth: memory

Time runs out long before memory

Memory grows with N, the time per step with N². So the largest N that memory allows lies far beyond the largest N that time allows. Without tiles in the force kernel it would be the other way round.

6 · In depth: memory

How the force kernel works through its block in tiles

A rank computes the forces on its own N/P stars from all N stars: a block of N/P × N pairs. NumPy needs temporary arrays for every pair it handles at once. Without tiles that is the whole block; with tiles it is a few rows at a time, and only one tile's temporaries exist at any moment.

—

P = 24 on one node. A tile is 218 pairs; its temporaries take 79 B per pair as measured (56 B with a del at the end of the tile loop). The rest of the rank is the start-up and the catalogue, from the memory model.

7 · Load balance after merging

When stars merge, hand the survivors out evenly again

Merging removes stars. If every rank keeps the stars it owned, the rank that lost the most has less to do and waits, while the ranks with the most stars set the pace of every step. Handing the survivors out evenly again costs nothing extra here, because every rank already holds the full state.

handed out again at once ownership kept: most stars on a rank ownership kept: fewest stars on a rank

Rasmus' experiment (experiments/): 1 000 Gaia stars, 100 steps, 12 prescribed pairs merged at steps 10, 20, …, 80, four ranks on one node, five repeats per policy in one job. The merges are concentrated in one rank's block on purpose: an unfavourable case for keeping ownership.

9 · Conclusion

The final configuration

DecisionWhy
v1: Allgatherv, blockingThe best or equal from N = 1 000 on, on one node and on two.
No overlap, no ringNothing moves in the background on one node, and the transfers are tiny next to the kernel; the ring's extra calls cost more than they save.
Whole nodes; avoid P = 32With 24 + 8 ranks the Allgatherv is 4–6 times slower than with 48 (N ≥ 5 000).
P by NN ≲ 100: about 8 ranks on one node. N ≈ 1 000: up to 48. N ≥ 5 000: 48 ranks.
Hand the stars out evenly after mergesIn Rasmus' test 5.7 % faster than keeping ownership, and free, because every rank holds the full state anyway.
No memory optimisationMemory suffices for millions of stars; time is the limit. Optional: a del in the tile loop takes the kernel from 79 to 56 B per pair.
Numba: a team decisionNot measured, but the only large lever on absolute speed: the Python work per step and per kernel call sets the floor at small N.

9 · Conclusion

What the measurements say, and what is still open

  1. The computation per step is the limit, not the network and not the memory.
  2. Expectations that did not hold: the Allgather did not hit a communication floor up to 48 ranks; 2D did not scale further; overlap did not help; the ring at N = 50 cost about 90 times what C0 predicted, because its cost is the P kernel calls per step.
  3. Open: weak scaling (the Gustafson framework is ready, nothing is measured yet); the physics checks of the project description (energy, two-body orbit, Plummer sphere); why the kernel slows down when every core is busy; a performance model that predicts where a communication floor would appear.
